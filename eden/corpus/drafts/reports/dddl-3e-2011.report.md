### WAL-CLM-DDDL-000128 · prevalence
- **essentials:** — · **conditions:** achlorhydria · **symptoms:** —
- **claim:** Achlorhydria — what causes it, and how common it is. Achlorhydria means the loss of the stomach's hydrochloric acid (HCl). Wallach names two causes, and says both run opposite to what most people assume. The first is stress — contrary to popular belief it does not raise stomach acid, it shuts acid production down, and he pins the moment: once stress passes the point of decompensation, where the body can no longer compensate. The second is age itself, a natural process he calls perhaps the most significant aging phenomenon, at 75 percent of people over 50 requiring supplemental stomach acid. A third contributor is dietary: ordinary salt (sodium chloride) is the raw material the stomach uses to manufacture its acid, so people on salt-restricted diets are more likely to become achlorhydric. The everyday sign is 'burp, belch, and bloat' after eating.

of 50 years require supplementation of stomach acid.”

### WAL-CLM-DDDL-000129 · prognosis
- **essentials:** — · **conditions:** dyspepsia, osteoporosis, anemia, food_allergy · **symptoms:** —
- **claim:** Chronic dyspepsia — what it costs if you leave it. Dyspepsia means poor digestion, and Wallach describes it by its everyday signs: belching, burping, bloating and 'acid stomach.' His explanation inverts the usual one — the trouble is not too much acid but that the stomach's acid is not acid enough, so food ferments and the gas is burped back up. He calls it probably the most common 'disease' in the western world and certainly the most costly, measured by what people spend on relief — but says the real cost is what it becomes: food allergies, osteoporosis, anemia, a debilitated immune system, degenerative disease. Prevention is reduced stress, exercise, healthful diet habits and reasonable food volumes; and because stomach-acid production falls as we age — an unforgiving event, and the main cause of dyspepsia in seniors — the regular use of digestive aids.

### WAL-CLM-DDDL-000130 · contraindication
- **essentials:** — · **conditions:** dyspepsia · **symptoms:** —
- **claim:** Antacids versus digestive aids. Wallach's position on antacids is split, and the split follows from his account of indigestion. In an emergency they are a good temporary fix. As a regular habit they harm you — regular use will eventually damage you by reducing the nutrients you absorb — because the stomach's acid is already not acid enough, so neutralizing it further undercuts the digestion and absorption that acid exists to drive. What he prescribes instead is replacement rather than suppression: 75 to 200 mg of betaine hydrochloride (a supplemental form of stomach acid) with pancreatic enzymes, three times a day, 15 to 30 minutes before each meal. His herbal preventions and remedies are papaya, common barberry, and bitters such as gentian — and in some cases a glass of wine or several ounces of vinegar before meals will help.

### WAL-CLM-DDDL-000131 · protocol
- **essentials:** — · **conditions:** — · **symptoms:** —
- **claim:** Who should take digestive aids, and why. Wallach gives this instruction as part of preventing candidiasis — the Candida albicans overgrowth he ties to chronic fatigue syndrome — and the reasoning is what makes the advice land. Candida is a normally harmless yeast living on the skin, mouth, vagina and intestine. It turns pathological when the gut's competing normal bacteria are killed off by long-term antibiotic use, or when low stomach acid (hypochlorhydria, usually the result of salt restriction) lets it move up into the stomach — a location it does not normally occupy. Stress reduces stomach acid once it reaches the point of decompensation, and food allergies change the lining of the gut in a way very similar to celiac disease, raising the nutrient concentration in the gut into a 'hyperfertilized' environment the yeast finds attractive; eventually the overgrowth spills into the bloodstream. So his prevention starts with knowing your own digestive and absorptive patterns: pulse-test for food allergies, avoid long-term antibiotics, and as you grow older take betaine HCl and pancreatic enzymes before meals to offset the normal decrease in stomach-acid production. He is emphatic on two more points — take these digestive aids if you are under stress, and deal aggressively with any disease that lowers your immune capacity.

### WAL-CLM-DDDL-000132 · mechanism
- **essentials:** — · **conditions:** bloating, hypochlorhydria · **symptoms:** —
- **claim:** Gastric bloating — where the gas actually comes from. Bloating is the accumulation of gas in the stomach, and Wallach's explanation turns on a fact most people don't know: a healthy stomach is sterile, and it stays that way because it is so acidic. When hypochlorhydria — low stomach acid — sets in, that sterility fails, and bacteria and yeast from the small intestine migrate upward into the stomach, a place they do not belong. There they ferment the carbohydrates and sugars you have eaten, and the gas that fermentation produces is the 'bloat.' It is the same picture he draws for achlorhydria, where the symptom triad is 'burp, belch, and bloat,' and it explains why his treatment goes after the missing acid — colloidal minerals, betaine HCl and pancreatic enzymes before meals — rather than after the gas.
- **verbatim** (offset 650581): “BLOATING (gastric) is the accumulation of gas in the stomach. Normally
the stomach is sterile because of the acid environment; however, when
hypochlorhydria (low stomach acid) occurs, bacteria and yeast from the
small intestine migrates up into the stomach. The bacteria in the stomach
now “ferments” carbohydrates and sugars that are eaten and produce gas or
“bloat.””

### WAL-CLM-DDDL-000133 · mechanism
- **essentials:** — · **conditions:** dumping_syndrome, anemia, osteoporosis · **symptoms:** —
- **claim:** Dumping syndrome — why an acid intestine disables your enzymes. Dumping syndrome is the sudden emptying of stomach contents into the small intestine all at once, overloading the intestine's natural buffer system. That matters because of where the body's acid is meant to sit: the stomach is supposed to be strongly acidic, the small intestine is not, and pancreatic enzymes require an alkaline environment to function — so when unbuffered acid floods in, they stop working properly. Wallach notes it is a common side effect of stomach surgery, and that anemia and osteoporosis are its common secondary diseases, which is what you would expect from a stretch of gut that can no longer digest and absorb. His treatment works with the mechanism rather than against it: any of the classic bitters such as gentian, folic acid at 3 to 5 mg a day, pectin at half an ounce in water before meals, high animal-protein diets, six to eight small meals a day rather than a few large ones, and lying down for half an hour after eating.
- **verbatim** (offset 736305): “DUMPING SYNDROME is the sudden “dumping” of stomach contents
into the small intestine all at once, thus overloading the natural intestinal
buffer system. The resulting acid condition in the intestine prevents proper
functioning of pancreatic enzymes, which require an alkaline environment.
Dumping syndrome is a common side effect of stomach surgery. Anemia
and osteoporosis are common secondary diseases of the dumping
syndrome.”

### WAL-CLM-DDDL-000134 · definition
- **essentials:** — · **conditions:** — · **symptoms:** —
- **claim:** How acidic your stomach is supposed to be. Wallach draws a sharp line between the body's internal chemistry and its secretions. Internally, acidity is policed within a very narrow range: healthy blood runs 7.36 to 7.44, below 7.30 is acidosis, above 7.44 is alkalosis, and anything below 6.8 or above 7.8 is rapidly fatal. Secretions and excretions are the opposite — deliberately variable, running from about 1.0 in stomach acid at one end to 8.2 in pancreatic juice at the other, with alkaline urine in vegans. That spread is the point rather than a flaw. The stomach is meant to be strongly acidic: it is the acid environment that keeps the stomach sterile and that activates protein digestion. The pancreatic juice meeting food further down the tract is meant to be alkaline — which is why acid conditions in the small intestine disable pancreatic enzymes.
- **verbatim** (offset 535386): “In contrast to the internal body, the pH
of secretions and excretions can be more variable and range from 1.0 in
stomach acid to 8.2 in pancreatic juice and alkaline urine in vegans.”
