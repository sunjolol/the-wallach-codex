- **claim:** Helium — what it is. Helium is the lightest of the 'noble gases,' the column of chemically inert elements at the edge of the periodic table. It is so inert that it will not react or combine even with itself and forms no stable compounds, so it occurs in nature as a pure element. After hydrogen, helium is the second-most-abundant element in the universe: it makes up about seven percent of all atoms, and together helium and hydrogen account for 99.9% of the matter in the universe. Most of Earth's helium is trapped in 'natural gas,' formed as radioactive uranium and radon decay (the released helium nuclei are the 'alpha particles' named by Ernest Rutherford). Helium was the first element ever discovered off the Earth: in 1868 the French astronomer Pierre Janssen saw an unexplained line in the sun's spectrum and named the new element from the Greek Helios ('sun'); the Scottish chemist William Ramsay isolated it from uranium ore in 1895. In his words: "Helium is colorless, odorless and is so inert that it will not even react or combine with itself. It is so inert that it is one of those few elements that are found in nature in its pure elemental form. No stable compound of helium has ever been manufactured. Helium is second only to hydrogen as the most abundant element in the universe. Helium makes up seven percent of all the existing atoms. Together, helium and hydrogen make up 99.9% of elements in the universe."

- **claim:** Helium and 'the bends' (decompression sickness). Deep-sea divers breathe a helium-oxygen mixture instead of ordinary air because helium is far less soluble in blood than the nitrogen in air, so less gas dissolves under pressure and the risk of 'the bends' drops. The bends is caused by a diver surfacing too quickly: the sudden drop in pressure makes the gases dissolved in the blood bubble out of solution — like the fizzing of a just-opened bottle of carbonated soda — and in the body those bubbles become trapped in the joints, causing severe pain that doubles the diver over into a fetal position. In his words: "Divers use helium/ oxygen 'air' when working at great depths; helium being less soluble than nitrogen dissolves at a low percentage in blood, therefore reducing the risk of 'bends.' The 'bends' is a condition that is produced by a diver coming to the surface too quickly, the lower pressure at the water's surface results in the dissolved gasses to bubble out of solution. The effect is like fizzing of an opened bottle or can of carbonated soft drinks. In the human body these gas bubbles become trapped in the joints, causing severe pain and causing the diver to 'bend' in a fetal position."

- **claim:** Hafnium — a common metal that hid in plain sight. Hafnium is a bright, silvery-white metal that is actually common and abundant in the Earth's crust, yet it was not discovered until 1923 — unusually late — because it is almost always found together with its chemical 'twin' zirconium and is nearly impossible to separate from it, so zirconium long masked its existence. Mendeleyev and other investigators had predicted a missing 'element 72.' The Dutch physicist Dirk Coster and the Hungarian physicist George de Hevesy finally isolated it and named it Hafnia — the Latin name for Copenhagen — to honor the home city of Niels Bohr. Hafnium is denser than zirconium but chemically almost identical to it. In his words: "Hafnium is a common and abundant metal, however, it was not discovered until 1923. Numerous investigators, including the creator of the periodic table Mendeleyev, predicted the existence of 'element 72,' however, the ever present diamond-like twin zirconium overshadowed the existence of hafnium. Ultimately, Dirk Coster, a Dutch physicist and George Karl von Hevesy, a Hungarian physicist found the element."
