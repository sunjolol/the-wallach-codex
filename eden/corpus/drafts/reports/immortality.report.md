### WAL-CLM-IMMORT-000119 · mechanism
- **essentials:** — · **conditions:** tooth_decay · **symptoms:** —
- **claim:** How fluoride protects teeth — the mechanism behind fluoride toothpaste. Tooth enamel is made of a hard mineral, hydroxyapatite. Acids that mouth bacteria produce from sugary food and drink slowly dissolve it, and that loss of enamel starts tooth decay ('caries'). The body constantly fights back by rebuilding enamel (remineralization). When sodium fluoride is present, the rebuilt enamel forms as fluorapatite — a version considerably more resistant to acid damage than natural enamel. That acid-resistance is exactly why manufacturers add sodium fluoride to toothpaste. In his words: "The resulting loss of the hydroxyapatite enamel promotes the initiation of tooth decay or 'caries.' One of the body's defenses against the breakdown of hydroxyapatite is to generate new enamel continuously (remineralization). In the presence of sodium fluoride, the remineralization process manufactures a form of enamel referred to as fluorapatite. Fluorapatite is considerably more resistant to damage from acid than the natural enamel."

- **claim:** The fluoride-cancer studies Wallach cites. Epidemiological work by Yiamouyiannis and Burk in 1977 led to full-scale U.S. congressional hearings over the charge that fluoridation of public water systems had caused 10,000 excess cancer deaths. ### WAL-CLM-IMMORT-000124 · definition
- **essentials:** — · **conditions:** — · **symptoms:** —
- **claim:** Wallach's full stance on fluoride — why it sounds both essential and dangerous, made clear. WHAT IT IS: fluoride is the most reactive halogen and, per a 1972 study by Schwarz, a nutritionally essential trace mineral for animals and humans. ITS REAL BENEFIT: fluoride hardens tooth enamel — teeth rebuild it as acid-resistant fluorapatite, which fights the cavities caused by sugar-fed mouth bacteria; that is why it's in toothpaste, and water fluoridation (~1 mg/L) has reportedly cut cavities 60–70%. ITS REAL HARMS: fluoride is also toxic above trace levels — dental fluorosis (mottled teeth) at 2–7 ppm, osteosclerosis (abnormal bone hardening) at 8–20 ppm, and chronic systemic toxicity above 20–80 ppm/day over years — and Wallach cites studies (Yiamouyiannis & Burk 1977; the U.S. National Toxicology Program's 1990 rat study) tying fluoridation to excess cancer deaths and to oral, bone (osteosarcoma), thyroid and liver tumors. HOW IT'S BOTH — the reconciliation: the dose makes the poison. Fluoride is essential only in tiny amounts (the whole adult skeleton holds ~2.6 g; average intake ~4.4 mg/day), while the harms appear at higher, sustained exposure — so 'essential in trace' and 'toxic in excess' are the same mineral at different doses, not a contradiction. ON WATER FLUORIDATION: Wallach calls it 'still highly controversial' and presents both sides — the cavity benefit versus the fluorosis, bone and cancer concerns, even noting that studies disagree on whether fluoridated water reduces or increases bone fractures. In this passage he lays out the debate and flags the toxicity/cancer evidence rather than issuing a flat yes-or-no verdict on whether you should drink it. In his words: "The universal fluoridation of drinking water is still highly controversial. Some studies show that fluoridated water helps reduce fractures from osteoporosis, while other studies showed an increase in hip fractures."
