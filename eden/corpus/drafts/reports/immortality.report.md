### WAL-CLM-IMMORT-000068 · mechanism
- **essentials:** calcium · **conditions:** celiac_disease, lactose_intolerance, hypochlorhydria · **symptoms:** —
- **claim:** How well calcium is absorbed depends heavily on its form and the gut environment. It is taken up mainly in the duodenum while the contents are still acidic; once the intestine turns alkaline, absorption falls off. Plain metallic calcium may be only 1-3% absorbed, whereas an organically bound, plant-derived colloidal or water-soluble chelated form can reach up to 98%. Wallach lists the causes of a calcium deficiency as: too little vitamin D; low stomach acid (hypochlorhydria, which can follow a salt-restricted diet); lactose intolerance; celiac disease; a high-fat or low-protein diet; and heavy fiber and phytate intake. Phytic acid (in grain bran, oatmeal, whole wheat) and oxalic acid (in rhubarb, spinach, chard, mustard greens) each bind calcium into insoluble, unabsorbable salts, and excess sugar, carbonation and caffeine leach stored calcium from the bones. In his words: "Causes of a calcium deficiency can include, a deficiency or lack of vitamin D; the lack of stomach acid or hypochlorhydria can result from a restricted NaCl (salt) intake; lactose intolerance; celiac disease; high fat diet; low protein diet; and a high fiber and phytate consumption."

### WAL-CLM-IMMORT-000069 · mechanism

- **claim:** The body holds blood calcium within a tight range using two hormones. Parathormone (from the parathyroid glands) and calcitonin (from the thyroid) keep serum calcium at 8.5-10.5 mg/dL, largely by drawing on the calcium reserves in bone. Parathormone raises blood calcium — pulling it from bone, making the kidney retain more, and making the gut absorb more efficiently; when blood calcium climbs above 10.5 mg/dL from too much parathyroid activity, calcitonin reduces how much calcium is released from the bones. ### WAL-CLM-IMMORT-000071 · mechanism
- **essentials:** calcium · **conditions:** hypertension · **symptoms:** —
- **claim:** Wallach ties chronic calcium deficiency to high blood pressure. In 1980 McCarron and colleagues theorized that chronic calcium deficiency leads to hypertension, and more than 30 later studies supported it; serum ionized calcium runs consistently lower in people with untreated hypertension. The link is clearest in people taking in less than 500 mg of calcium a day. In salt-sensitive hypertension, a rise in blood pressure tracks with urinary calcium loss from excess salt on top of an existing calcium deficiency — and among Black and elderly white people with essential hypertension, low calcium and potassium (rather than high salt) drive the "salt sensitivity." In a four-year study of 58,218 nurses, hypertension was more likely in women taking in less than 800 mg of calcium per day. In his words: "In 1980, McCarron et al theorized that chronic calcium deficiency probably led to hypertension. More than 30 subsequent studies supported the original theory of calcium deficiency as the cause of hypertension. In addition, recent studies have shown that serum ionized calcium is consistently lower in humans with untreated hypertension."
