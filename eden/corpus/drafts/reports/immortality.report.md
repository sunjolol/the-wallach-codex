### WAL-CLM-IMMORT-000126 · mechanism
- **essentials:** iron · **conditions:** — · **symptoms:** —
- **claim:** Why iron is essential — it is what lets your blood carry oxygen. Iron is an essential nutrient for all land animals. Each hemoglobin molecule (the oxygen-carrying protein of red blood cells) is built around four iron atoms; oxygen chemically bonds to those iron atoms in the tiny blood vessels of the lungs (capillaries) and is then carried through the circulation to cells throughout the body. Iron was first recognized as an essential animal nutrient by Boussingault in the 1860s. In his words: "Iron is an essential nutrient for all terrestrial species of animals. It is the four iron atoms in each hemoglobin molecule that are central to its structure and physiological function. Oxygen actually bonds to the iron atoms in the lung capillaries, and then are transported via the circulation to distant cells."

### WAL-CLM-IMMORT-000127 · mechanism

- **claim:** Why carbon monoxide is so deadly — it hijacks iron's oxygen seat. Carbon monoxide, a gas produced by incomplete burning of fuel, binds to the iron in hemoglobin about 200 times more tightly than oxygen does. Because it clings so hard, it crowds oxygen out of the blood — which is what makes carbon monoxide poisoning dangerous. In his words: "Carbon monoxide, a byproduct of imperfect combustion, is bound to the hemoglobin iron 200 times more tightly than oxygen - this phenomenon can lead to 'carbon monoxide poisoning!'"

### WAL-CLM-IMMORT-000128 · prevalence
- **essentials:** iron · **conditions:** — · **symptoms:** —
- **claim:** How much iron your body holds, and where it sits. A healthy adult carries about 3 to 5 grams of iron, and a newborn has nearly twice as much per kilogram of body weight as an adult. Roughly 60 to 70% is 'functional' iron doing active work — in hemoglobin, in muscle's oxygen-storing pigment myoglobin, and in the cell's respiratory enzymes — while 30 to 40% is held in reserve as storage iron (as ferritin and hemosiderin in the bone marrow and liver). In his words: "In a healthy adult human there is three to five grams of iron, the newborn infant has nearly double the amount of iron per kg than adults. Sixty to 70% of tissue iron is used as essential or functional iron, and 30 to 40% as storage iron."

Wallach lists the drivers: an unsupplemented pregnancy; painful or heavy menstruation (dysmenorrhea); chronic infections; low stomach acid (hypochlorhydria, e.g. from a salt-restricted diet); chronic diarrhea; ongoing blood loss (from cancer, ulcers, parasites, blood-thinning drugs, or other nutritional deficiencies); and poor absorption (from high-fat or high-fiber diets, or celiac disease). ### WAL-CLM-IMMORT-000132 · interaction
- **essentials:** iron · **conditions:** — · **symptoms:** —
- **claim:** What helps and hinders iron absorption — and why the standard allowance can fall short. Stomach acid (hydrochloric acid) is needed to absorb iron well, and vitamin C (ascorbic acid) increases absorption; clays and dietary fiber (phytates) reduce it. Because of that, Wallach argues the official RDA (Recommended Dietary Allowance) of 18 mg per day of metallic iron is too low for people eating high-fiber, high-phytate diets, such as vegans and vegetarians. In his words: "Stomach hydrochloric acid is required for optimal absorption of iron. Ascorbic acid increases the absorption of iron; clays and dietary fiber (phytates) decrease the absorption of iron. The RDA of 18 mg per day as metallic iron is too low for those individuals consuming high fiber, high phytate diets (vegans, vegetarians, etc.)."

### WAL-CLM-IMMORT-000133 · toxicity_sign
- **essentials:** iron · **conditions:** cirrhosis, pancreatic_fibrosis, diabetes, heart_failure · **symptoms:** —
- **claim:** Too much iron can harm you — but Wallach says not by direct poisoning. Excess iron can lead to cirrhosis of the liver, fibrosis (scarring) of the pancreas, diabetes, and heart failure. His key point: these are not caused by iron being directly toxic — instead, the extra iron drives up the body's need for other minerals (selenium, chromium, vanadium, copper, zinc), and it is that induced shortfall that does the damage. In his words: "Excesses of iron can result in cirrhosis of the liver, fibrosis of the pancreas, diabetes and heart failure. These diseases are not the direct result of toxic affects of iron, but rather, the increased iron consumption produces an increased need for selenium, chromium, vanadium, copper, zinc, etc."

- **claim:** Gallium — the metal that melts in your hand. Gallium is in the same chemical family as aluminum and behaves much like it. It is so soft you can cut it with a knife, and its melting point is so low (29.8 degrees C, about 86 degrees F) that it turns liquid from the warmth of your hand; yet it does not boil until 2,403 degrees C, giving it the widest liquid range of any known metal. Its existence was predicted by Mendeleyev's periodic table in 1871, and Paul-Emile Lecoq de Boisbaudran identified it in 1875, naming it for Gallia (the Latin name for France). In his words: "It is an extremely soft metal that can easily be cut with a knife. It also has an extremely low melting point of 29.8 degrees C, allowing gallium to liquefy when it is held in the hand. Gallium has an extremely high boiling point of 2,403 degrees C, allowing gallium to be in its liquid form in a range greater than that of any known metal."
