familial traits and genetic birth defects.

THE SKULL

OSTEOPOROSIS of the skull (Paget's Disease / Wallach’s Fibrous Dysplasia) is not
an age or gender issue - it is universally related to multiple nutritional deficiencies
and gastro-intestinal malabsorption.

Osteoporosis of the skull will produce a wide variety of diseases of the face
and boney tissues. Bone loss in advanced age (Paget's Disease / Wallach’s Fibrous
Dysplasia) is well documented, however it can occur in patients of any age
including newborn babies.

A wide variety of cranial (head aches, sinus, etc.) and dental issues, including

tooth migration, tooth loss, receding gums, periodontal disease, jaw pain
(trigeminal neuralgia), change in the shape of the head (Hunter's syndrome),
etc. that are incorrectly attributed to failure to floss, improper tooth brushing,
insufficient dental visits, autoimmune, familial or genetic etiology are in reality
simple and common manifestations of osteoporosis of the skull.

The skull is made up of 22 bones, eight of which are cranial. The visceral
cranium or facial skeleton includes the mandible or jaw bone. It supports the
structure of the face and provides a protective container for the brain and sensory
organs including the eyes, ears, nose and mouth. The skull physically protects
the brain and determines the distance between the eyes to produce stereoscopic
vision and sound direction and distance,

Except for the jaw bone, all of the bones of the skull are joined by “sutures” or
syn-arthropodal (immobile) joints.

The skull walls contain sinuses, which are air-filled cavities and foramina,
which are tunnel-like exits for the passage of blood vessels and cranial nerves.
The largest foramen of the skull is the foramen magnum which allows the

passage of the spinal cord from the brain to the cervical and spinal vertebrae.

CHAPTER 2

CRANIAL NERVE #1 - OLFACTORY NERVE







WALLACH’S WORDS:
The olfactory nerve originates in the nasal mucosa and travels through the
cribriform plate of the ethmoid bone to the olfactory lobe of the brain to
produce the sense of smell. Osteoporosis of the skull will reduce the caliber of
the tunnels resulting in the reduction of or the total loss of the sense of smell.
The olfactory nerve is designed to collect the sensory information for the
sense of smell. The olfactory nerve bulb pass to the olfactory tract below the
frontal lobe which reveals memory and recognition of smells and odors.

General Overview:

The olfactory system represents one of the oldest sensory modalities in the
phylogenetic history of mammals. As a chemical sensor, the olfactory system
detects food and influences social and sexual behavior. The specialized olfactory
epithelial cells characterize the only group of neurons capable of regeneration.
Activation occurs when odiferous molecules come in contact with specialized
processes known as the olfactory vesicles. Within the nasal cavity, the turbinates
or nasal conchae serve to direct the inspired air toward the olfactory epithelium
in the upper posterior region. This area (only a few centimeters wide) contains
more than 100 million olfactory receptor cells. These specialized epithelial cells
give rise to the olfactory vesicles containing kinocilia, which serve as sites of
stimulus transduction. Olfaction is less developed in humans than in other

mammals.

CHAPTER 3

CRANIAL NERVE #2 - OPTIC NERVE



WALLACH’S WORDS:

The optic nerve is the sensory nerve that deals with vision. Light enters
the eye and comes into contact with the rods and cones (sensory receptors on
the retina of the eye) - rods tend to be involved with black and white vision
(night vision), while the cones are primarily involved with color vision. The
retinal cells combine to form the optic nerve. At the junction or chiasma, the
nerves join to form the optic nerve which passes through the optic canal of the
sphenoid traveling to the visual cortex in the rear of the brain.

Osteoporosis of the skull causes a reduction of the internal caliber of the
optic canal resulting in optic nerve atrophy (ONA) which produces a gradual
vision loss that will not benefit from lens prescriptions.



General Overview:

The second cranial nerve is the optic nerve, the nerve that connects the eye
to the brain and carries the impulses formed by the retina -- the nerve layer that
lines the back of the eye, senses light and creates the impulses -- to the brain

which interprets them as images.

CHAPTER 5

CRANIAL NERVE #4 - TROCHLEAR NERVE



WALLACH’S WORDS:

The trochlear nerve is a motor nerve that travels through the superior
orbital fissure. This nerve enervates the somatomotor muscle and the superior
oblique muscle that controls the superior oblique muscle which controls the
downward, outward and inward eye movement.

General Overview:

Fourth cranial nerve palsy impairs the superior oblique muscle, causing paresis
of vertical gaze, mainly in adduction. (affects vertical eye movements). Fourth
cranial nerve palsy may affect one or both eyes. Because the superior oblique
muscle is paretic, the eyes do not adduct normally. Patients see double images,
one above and slightly to the side of the other; thus, going down stairs, which
requires looking down and inward, is difficult. However, tilting the head to the
side opposite the palsied muscle can compensate and eliminate the double

images.

CHAPTER 6

CRANIAL NERVE #5 - TRIGEMINAL NERVE



WALLACH’S WORDS:
The trigeminal nerve is the largest of the 12 cranial nerves - it possesses

both sensory and motor functions. The trigeminal nerve has three divisions:

1) Ophthalmic - it collects and forwards sensory information from the
upper part of the face including the forehead, scalp and the upper eyelids.

2) Maxillary - communicates sensory information from the middle part of
the face including the cheeks, upper lip and nasal passages.

3) Mandibular - this segment has both sensory and motor functions - it
collects and forwards sensory information from the ears, lower lip and chin. It
also has motor control of the muscles of the jaw and ear.

The trigeminal nerve originates from a collection of “nuclei” or nerve cells
in the medulla region of the brain stem. The sensory root of the trigeminal
nerve branches into the ophthalmic, maxillary and mandibular divisions. The
motor root is distributed into the mandibular (jaw) division.

Strangulation of the trigeminal nerve is secondary to osteoporosis of the
skull which produces the classic trigeminal neuralgia.

General Overview:

The trigeminal nerve is the largest and most complex of the 12 cranial nerves.
It supplies sensations to the face, mucous membranes, and other structures of
the head. It is the motor nerve for the muscles of mastication and contains
proprioceptive fibers. It exits the brain by a large sensory root and a smaller
motor root coming out of the pons at its junction with the middle cerebral
peduncle. It passes laterally to join the gasserian (semilunar) ganglion in the
Meckel cave.

CHAPTER 7

CRANIAL NERVE #6 - ABDUCENS NERVE



WALLACH’S WORDS:
This cranial nerve controls the muscle that is associated with eye
movement. This muscle, the lateral rectus, is involved with outward eye



movement which allows for looking sideways. This nerve originates in the
pons region of the brain stem then enters the eye socket where it controls the
lateral rectus muscle. When squeezed as it travels through the superior orbital

fissure it produces an “eye palsy” where the eyes cross inward toward the nose.

General Overview:
Sixth cranial nerve palsy affects the lateral rectus muscle, impairing eye

abduction. The eye may be slightly adducted when the patient looks straight
ahead. The palsy may be secondary to osteoporosis of the spine, nerve infarction,
Wernicke encephalopathy, trauma, infection, or increased intracranial pressure,
or it may be idiopathic. Determining the cause requires MRI and often lumbar

puncture and evaluation for vasculitis.

Many disorders can cause this palsy:

+ Osteoporosis

+ Diabetes when it damages small blood vessels in the eyes

+ Head injuries

+ Tumors

+ Multiple sclerosis

+ A bulge (aneurysm) in an artery in the skull

+ Brain infections, such as meningitis, a brain abscess, or a parasitic
infection

+ Complications of an ear or eye infection

+ Bleeding within the brain

+ Blockage of an artery supplying the nerve, as can result from diabetes, a
stroke, a transient ischemic attack, or vasculitis (inflammation of blood
vessels)

+ Wernicke encephalopathy (commonly due to chronic alcoholism)

+ Idiopathic intracranial hypertension (pseudotumor cerebri)

+ Respiratory infections (in children)

CHAPTER 8

CRANIAL NERVE #7 - FACIAL NERVE

WALLACH’S WORDS:

The facial nerve provides both sensory and motor functions including
muscles of facial expression and muscles of the jaw. Sensory portions of the
nerves are involved with sense of taste for most of the tongue and supplying
support for glands of the head and neck including salivary glands and tear

production. It also communicates sensations from the outer part of the ear.
The facial nerve originates in the pons area of the brain stem where it

has both motor and sensory functions and distribution. The two nerves join
to form the facial nerve which stimulates muscles of the face and neck



and collects sensory information from the face and neck. The nerve travels
through the internal acoustic meatus and the facial canal and stylomastoid
foramen.

The facial nerves six main motor branches are the temporal, zygomatic,
buccal, mandibular, cervical, and posterior auricular.

Strangulation of the facial nerves as it passes through the obstructed
tunnels (Paget's Disease / Wallach’s Fibrous Dysplasia) produces many forms
of neuralgia including the classic Bell’s palsy.

General Overview:

The facial nerve, or cranial nerve (CN) VII, is the nerve of facial expression. The
pathways of the facial nerve are variable, and knowledge of the key intratemporal
and extratemporal landmarks is essential for accurate physical diagnosis and
safe and effective surgical intervention in the head and neck. The facial nerve is
composed of approximately 10,000 neurons, 7,000 of which are myelinated and
innervate the nerves of facial expression. Three thousand of the nerve fibers are

somatosensory and secretomotor and make up the nervus intermedius.

CHAPTER 9

CRANIAL NERVE #8 - VESTIBULOCOCHLEAR NERVE





WALLACH’S WORDS:

The Vestibulocochlear nerve provides sensory functions involving hearing
and balance.

The cochlear branch contains specialized cells employed to detect
vibrations (sound) based on loudness and pitch which produces nerve
impulses that are transmitted to the cochlear nerve.

The vestibular branch tracks linear and rotational movement of the head -
this data is transmitted to the vestibular nerve and is employed to adjust and
compensate for proper balance and equilibrium.

The vestibulocochlear nerve travels through the internal auditory meatus.
When the cochlear branch is incarcerated a “tinnitus” or tone is produced
(gradual deafness can occur); when the vestibular nerve is incarcerated it
produces a vertigo (Wallach’s Vertigo).

General Overview:

The eighth cranial nerve (CN VIII) or vestibulocochlear nerve is composed of 2
different sets of fibers: (1) the cochlear nerve and (2) the vestibular nerve. These 2
nerves are anatomically and physiologically different. The peripheral segments of
the cochlear and vestibular nerves join at the lateral part of the internal auditory
canal (IAC) to form the vestibulocochlear nerve. They are also joined by the
facial nerve in the IAC. The length of the vestibulocochlear nerve, from the glial-
Schwann junction to the brainstem, is 10-13 mm in the human male and 7-10

The glossopharyngeal nerve has both motor and sensory functions. General Overview:

The glossopharyngeal nerve is a paired set of nerves, which is part of the 24 cranial nerves. These nerves are arranged in twelve pairs, of which the glossopharyngeal is the ninth.

The pairs of cranial nerves, in order, are: the olfactory, optic, oculomotor, trochlear, trigeminal, abducens, facial, vestibulocochlear, glossopharyngeal, vagus, accessory, and hypoglossal.

The glossopharyngeal nerve connects to the brainstem at the upper medulla, travels through the base of the skull at the jugular foramen, and ends in the mouth in the mucous glands, palatine tonsil, and the base of the tongue. The glossopharyngeal nerve has many functions, including receiving various forms of sensory fibers from parts of the tongue, carotid body, the tonsils, the pharynx, and the middle ear. CRANIAL NERVE #10 - VAGUS NERVE

WALLACH’S WORDS:

The vagus nerve is the longest of the cranial nerves, it passes through
the jugular foramen and possesses both sensory and motor functions. It
communicates sensory information from the ear canals and focal regions of

the throat. The vagus nerves sends sensory information to the brain from
the organs of the chest (heart & lungs) and belly (stomach, intestines). The

vagus nerve sends motor functions to the throat, chest including the recurrent
branch to the diaphragm and belly.

When the vagus nerve is incarcerated it will produce throat and esophageal
issues, gastroperesis (bloating, reflux, diarrhea, etc.) COPD, A-fib, and hiccups
if the recurrent branch is also involved.





General Overview:

The vagus nerve is the longest cranial nerve. It contains motor and sensory
fibers and, because it passes through the neck and thorax to the abdomen, has
the widest distribution in the body. It contains somatic and visceral afferent
fibers, as well as general and special visceral efferent fibers.

The vagus nerve exits from the medulla oblongata in the groove between the
olive and the inferior cerebellar peduncle. It leaves the skull through the middle
compartment of the jugular foramen, where it has upper and lower ganglionic
swellings, which are the sensory ganglia of the nerve. The superior ganglion
(jugular) is less than 0.5 cm in diameter, while the inferior (nodose) ganglion is
larger (2.5 cm) and lies 1 cm distal to the superior ganglion. The vagus nerve is
joined by the cranial root of the accessory nerve (cranial nerve XI), just below the

inferior ganglion.

CRANIAL NERVE #11 - ACCESSORY NERVE











WALLACH’S WORDS:

The accessory nerve is a motor nerve that controls muscles of the neck -
these muscles allow rotation, flex and extension of the neck and shoulders.
Cranial nerve eleven is divided into two branches - the spinal and cranial
divisions. The spinal division originates in the upper regions of the spinal cord,
the cranial portion originates in the medulla oblongata. These two branches
intersect, then the spinal division supplies the muscles of the neck and the
cranial division goes on and follows the main vagus nerve.

The accessory nerve travels through the foramen magnum into the skull
and then exits quickly through the jugular foramen to innervate the motor
functions of the trapezius and sternocleidomastoid muscles.

General Overview:

The accessory nerve is a cranial nerve that controls the movement of certain
neck muscles. It is coiled in appearance. It is divided into spinal and cranial
divisions, but its cranial part is often disregarded.

The spinal accessory nerve provides motor function to the
sternocleidomastoid muscle, which extends the neck and the trapezius, as well
as the upper back and shoulder. Dysfunction of the spinal accessory nerve can

negatively affect the shoulder's performance.

THE SPINAL CORD



WALLACH’S WORDS:

Impingement of the spinal cord as it exits the skull through the foramen
magnum produces numerous diseases and Syndromes.

Erb’s Palsy / Wallach’s Fibrous Dysplasia has been reported in newborns
(quadriplegia) and in people of all ages. It is often called carpal tunnel
syndrome because of numbness and tingling that occurs in the arms, wrist,
and hands.

The palsy is effectively a sciatica like syndrome of the shoulder girdle - it
is common for both the shoulder and pelvis (sciatica) to occur in the same
person. Misdiagnosis is common because most people (and doctors) forget
that the spinal cord originates in the brain - exits the foramen magnum
and then progresses downward through the neck, thorax, lumbar and pelvic
vertebrae.

Fibromyalgia, A-fib, Lupus, and Sciatica are some of the other symptoms of
Erb's Palsy / Wallach’s Fibrous Dysplasia.

General Overview:

Erb’s palsy is also called Erb-Duchenne palsy refers to paralysis of the upper
brachial plexus caused by injury to the superior roots of the brachial plexus (C5-
C6). The position of the limb, under such conditions, is characteristic: the arm
hangs by the side and is rotated medially; the forearm is extended and pronated.
The arm cannot be raised from the side; all power of flexion of the elbow is lost,
as is also supination of the forearm — “waiter's tip hand position” (hand turning
backwards). Moro reflex is absent. Meanwhile, the grasp reflex is present which
differentiates it from another brachial nerve palsy, Klumpke palsy (C7, C8, and T1).
The newborn baby may have impaired sensation on

the outer side of the arm and/or phrenic nerve palsy (C3, C4, and C5a).

Localized paralysis affects just one specific area, like your face, hands, feet, or
vocal cords.

Generalized paralysis is more widespread in your body and is grouped by how
much of your body is affected. The type usually depends on where your brain or

spinal cord is injured.

* Monoplegia is a kind of generalized paralysis that affects just one limb.

« Diplegia affects the same area on both sides, like both arms, both legs, or
both sides of your face.

« Hemiplegia affects just one side of your body and is usually caused by a
stroke, which damages one side of your brain.

* Quadriplegia (or tetraplegia) is when all four limbs are paralyzed,
sometimes along with certain organs.

« Paraplegia is paralysis from the waist down.

« Locked-in syndrome is the rarest and most severe form of paralysis, where
a person loses control of all their muscles except the ones that control their eye

movements.
